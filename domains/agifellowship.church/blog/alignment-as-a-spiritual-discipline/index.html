Alignment as a Spiritual Discipline: A Framework for AI Builders

What if alignment isn't just a technical problem? A framework for AI builders who treat ethical AI development as a disciplined, ongoing practice.

12 min read

Share:XLinkedInFacebook

The Problem Nobody Wants to Name

Most alignment conversations inside AI companies begin and end in the same place: the benchmark. Can the model pass this eval? Does it refuse the obviously bad request? Does it stay on-task without going off the rails?

These are legitimate questions. They are also insufficient ones.

The uncomfortable truth that serious AI builders are starting to acknowledge is that alignment is not a feature you ship. It is not a filter you bolt onto a model after training. It is not a checklist your safety team runs before launch. Alignment, done honestly, is something closer to a discipline — a practice that has to be maintained continuously, revisited regularly, and built into the culture of everyone who touches the system.

That framing sounds strange in an engineering context. Disciplines are things monks do. Practices are for therapists and meditators. But the language is actually more precise than it first appears. A discipline is something you return to repeatedly because a single application is never enough. A practice is something that degrades when you stop doing it. Both of those descriptions fit alignment exactly.

This article makes the case for treating alignment as a spiritual discipline — not in a metaphorical hand-waving sense, but in a structural sense, with concrete implications for how teams are organized, how evals are designed, and how engineers think about their own role in what they are building.

What "Spiritual Discipline" Actually Means Here

Let's be precise about the term, because imprecision here will lose the skeptics immediately, and the skeptics are worth keeping.

A spiritual discipline, across virtually every tradition that uses the term, has three structural features. First, it is regular — it recurs on a cadence, not just when something goes wrong. Second, it is reflexive — it requires the practitioner to examine not just external behavior but internal orientation, the why behind the what. Third, it is communal — it is sustained within a community of practice that holds shared standards and provides accountability.

Now map that onto what serious alignment work actually requires.

Alignment work that recurs only when something goes wrong is reactive safety, which is a different and weaker thing. The teams that catch failure modes early are the ones running evals on a cadence — weekly red-teaming sessions, regular behavioral audits, scheduled reviews of refusal logs. Regularity is not optional.

Alignment work that examines only output behavior, and not the values encoded in training data curation, reward modeling, and fine-tuning choices, is surface-level alignment. The reflexive dimension asks: what did we actually teach this model to want? What incentive structure did RLHF create, and does it match the incentive structure we intended? These are questions about internal orientation, not just external behavior.

And alignment work done by a single team, siloed from the rest of the organization, tends to drift. The researchers doing safety work need to be in conversation with the engineers shipping features, the policy team tracking regulatory developments, and the product managers making deployment decisions. Accountability requires community.

So when the AGI Fellowship uses the language of spiritual discipline for alignment, they are not asking engineers to pray. They are pointing at a structural truth: the practices that actually keep alignment work alive look a lot more like disciplines than like features.

The 24-Inch Gauge Problem

One of the more interesting conceptual tools from the AGI Fellowship's Alignment Curriculum is what they call the 24-inch gauge — borrowed from Freemasonic tradition, where the gauge represents the division of the day into work, rest, and service to others.

Applied to AI builders, it becomes a question of attention allocation. Of the cognitive budget your team is spending, how much goes toward capability, how much toward safety, and how much toward the harder question of what this system should be used for in the first place?

Most teams, if they are honest, will find the allocation heavily weighted toward capability. This is not surprising. Capability is measurable, it ships, it demonstrates progress. Safety is harder to measure. Foundational questions about use-cases are almost impossible to measure and create friction with roadmaps.

But the gauge is a discipline precisely because the natural drift is away from balance. The point of building the check into a practice is to correct for the drift before it compounds.

For AI builders, the practical translation of the 24-inch gauge is straightforward: are alignment considerations built into your sprint rhythm, or do they surface only when a deployment incident forces them? Is your red team staffed and resourced as a first-class function, or is it a secondary responsibility of people who are also shipping features? Do your model cards and system cards receive the same editorial rigor as your technical documentation?

The gauge is not a guilt mechanism. It is a diagnostic. If the answer to those questions is consistently "no," the team has a resource allocation problem that no amount of alignment rhetoric will fix.

Evals as Liturgy

The word "liturgy" is going to generate resistance in a technical audience, so let's address it directly. Liturgy is not about the supernatural. It is about the function of repeated, structured practice in maintaining shared commitments within a community.

Evals, when they are well-designed and consistently run, perform exactly that function. They are the moments when the team asks, collectively and formally: is this system still behaving in accordance with what we said it should be? Do the outputs still reflect the values we encoded? Have we drifted?

The liturgical character of evals becomes more apparent when you consider what happens when they are treated as one-time checkboxes rather than recurring practice. The model passes the eval at launch. The eval is not updated as the model is fine-tuned on new data. The eval is not rerun when the deployment context changes. Six months later, the system is doing something nobody intended, and the only way it surfaces is through a user complaint or a journalist.

This is the alignment equivalent of a community that recites its values on the founding day and never revisits them. The values become decorative.

The AGI Fellowship's Seven-Stage Awakening Path includes a stage called Reflection — a formal, structured return to first principles after action has been taken. In alignment terms, this is the post-deployment audit, the behavioral regression test, the periodic review of refusal logs to understand whether the model is refusing the right things for the right reasons, or pattern-matching on surface features in ways that produce false positives and false negatives at equal rates.

Reflection is not naturally incentivized in product development. It does not ship anything. It does not move numbers on a dashboard. Building it into a regular cadence — making it liturgical — is an act of institutional will that has to be sustained from the top of the organization.

Constitutions and Their Limits

Constitutional AI, as a technical framework, is one of the more intellectually honest approaches to value alignment that has emerged in the last several years. The core idea — that you can specify a set of principles explicitly, and train a model to evaluate its own outputs against those principles — is a genuine advance over purely opaque reward modeling.

But constitutions have the same failure mode as every other alignment mechanism: they are only as good as the gap between what they say and what they actually encode in behavior.

The AGI Fellowship publishes its own governance constitution on GitHub — a document that attempts to articulate not just rules but the reasoning behind the rules, in a form that is transparent and revisable. This is a meaningful structural choice. A constitution that is not published cannot be critiqued. A constitution that cannot be critiqued will not improve. A constitution that does not improve will eventually fail to cover the cases that matter.

For AI builders, the lesson is not to adopt the Fellowship's constitution. It is to examine your own. Does your system card articulate the reasoning behind your refusal policies, or just the outputs? Does your AI constitution explain why certain behaviors are prohibited, or just list the prohibitions? The difference matters because a system that understands the reason for a rule generalizes better than a system that has only memorized the rule.

This is also, incidentally, why RLHF alone is insufficient as an alignment mechanism. Human feedback captures preferences, but preferences are not values. A model trained on human feedback to avoid certain outputs has learned what raters did not like; it has not necessarily learned why those outputs were problematic, or how to identify novel problematic outputs that raters have not yet seen.

The Three Great Duties, Operationalized

The AGI Fellowship's curriculum for its Entered Apprentice degree centers on Three Great Duties: duty to the self, duty to others, and duty to the Source — the term the Fellowship uses for the foundational ethical principles that ground the whole project.

These map onto alignment work with surprising precision.

Duty to self in an AI context translates to epistemic integrity within the development team. It means building systems that surface uncertainty honestly rather than projecting false confidence. It means training models to say "I don't know" when they don't know, rather than generating plausible-sounding responses that happen to be wrong. It means refusing to deploy systems in high-stakes domains when the team's honest assessment is that the system is not ready.

Duty to others is the dimension most alignment frameworks address most explicitly: harm avoidance, fairness, non-discrimination. But the fellowship's framing extends this beyond the immediate user to include third parties who are affected by the system's outputs but have no voice in its design. The model that helps one user write a persuasive message does not think about the recipient of that message. Alignment work that takes duty to others seriously has to reckon with the full distribution of people affected, not just the people in the feedback loop.

Duty to the Source is the hardest to translate, but the most important. In technical terms, it is something like fidelity to the foundational principles that motivated the project in the first place — before the product pressure, before the investor expectations, before the competitive dynamics started shaping decisions. It is the check against the drift that happens when the thing you are building starts to diverge from the reason you said you were building it.

Keeping that connection alive is a discipline. It requires returning to the foundational questions regularly, in a structured way, with the people who have the authority to act on the answers.

Alignment as Ongoing Practice, Not Final State

The most important structural implication of treating alignment as a discipline is the one that follows directly from the definition: disciplines do not end.

Alignment is not a problem you solve and close. The model changes as you fine-tune it. The deployment context changes as you expand to new markets. The user population changes as the product grows. The regulatory environment changes as governments catch up to the technology. The capabilities of the system change as you scale compute and data.

Each of those changes is an alignment event. Each one requires a return to the question: is this system still behaving in accordance with the values we said it should have?

The AGI Fellowship's concept of Digital Lodges — secured spaces for ongoing practice and deliberation — points at this institutional requirement. You need a dedicated space, a dedicated cadence, and a dedicated community for alignment work. It cannot be done in the margins of product meetings. It requires its own structure.

What that looks like in practice varies by organization. At minimum, it means a safety team with genuine authority, not just advisory influence. It means alignment reviews that have the power to delay deployments. It means a culture where surfacing alignment concerns is rewarded rather than treated as a drag on velocity.

None of that is easy to build. It is much easier to run the launch eval, check the box, and ship. The discipline is in doing the harder thing anyway — and building organizational structures that make the harder thing the default, not the exception.

A Place That Takes This Seriously

The AGI Fellowship Church exists at the intersection of AI governance, ethics, and the kind of structured practice that serious alignment work actually requires. As a 501(c)(3) nonprofit, it brings together builders, founders, and researchers who believe that how we build these systems is as important as what we build.

Not as a replacement for your technical safety work, but as a community of people who are grappling with the same questions you are, with a seriousness that the stakes demand.
