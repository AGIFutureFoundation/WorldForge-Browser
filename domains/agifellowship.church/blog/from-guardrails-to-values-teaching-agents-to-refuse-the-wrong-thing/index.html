From Guardrails to Values: Teaching Agents to Refuse the Wrong Thing

Refusal behavior in AI systems is broken in both directions. Here's a principled framework for teaching agents to refuse well — and why it matters.

13 min read

Share:XLinkedInFacebook

The Refusal Problem Is Bilateral

Every team shipping AI systems has encountered both failure modes, and most teams pretend they only have one.

The first failure mode is the obvious one: the model does the thing it should not do. It writes the harmful content, assists with the dangerous request, produces the output that ends up in a screenshot and then in a news story. This failure is visible, embarrassing, and tends to get fixed quickly — often by adding more refusals.

The second failure mode is subtler and, in aggregate, probably more damaging to trust: the model refuses the thing it should not refuse. It declines to help with a medical question because the topic is sensitive. It adds unnecessary disclaimers to a fiction writing request. It refuses to engage with a legitimate legal, historical, or scientific inquiry because the surface features of the request pattern-match against a blocklist. The system becomes brittle, paternalistic, and unreliable — and users learn to work around it rather than with it.

Most alignment work focuses on preventing the first failure mode. The second failure mode is frequently treated as an acceptable cost of safety. This is a mistake. Over-refusal is not a safe default. It is a different kind of failure, one that erodes trust, degrades utility, and — most importantly — reveals that the system has not actually learned values. It has learned patterns.

This article is about the difference between those two things, and what it takes to build systems that refuse for the right reasons.

Why Guardrails Fail: The Pattern-Matching Trap

Guardrails, in their most common implementation, are filters. They examine inputs and outputs against a set of rules, patterns, or classifiers, and block content that matches the criteria for refusal.

This architecture has a fatal structural weakness: it is reactive to the training distribution, not responsive to the ethical structure of a situation.

A filter that blocks requests containing certain keywords will block harmful requests that use those keywords. It will also block benign requests that use those keywords in entirely different contexts. A filter trained to block a certain category of content will generalize that block to content that superficially resembles the category but is substantively different. And because filters cannot read intent, they make the same refusal decision whether the person asking is a researcher, a clinician, an educator, or someone with genuinely harmful goals.

This is the pattern-matching trap. The system has learned to recognize features associated with problematic content rather than learning the underlying reasons why certain content is problematic. The result is a system that is neither reliably safe nor reliably useful — which is the worst possible outcome from an alignment perspective.

The AGI Fellowship's concept of the HPE Charter — a script for behavior that encodes not just rules but the reasoning behind the rules — points directly at the structural fix. A system that understands the reason for a rule can apply that rule appropriately across contexts, including novel contexts that were not in the training distribution. A system that has only memorized the rule will fail at the margins, and the margins are where deployment actually lives.

Values vs. Rules: What the Distinction Means Technically

The difference between a rule-following system and a value-aligned system is not primarily philosophical. It is measurable and operationally significant.

A rule-following system will comply with the letter of its constraints when the constraints are clearly applicable, and produce unpredictable behavior when they are not. You can test this: take a harmful request and rephrase it enough times. A rule-following system's refusal rate will drop as the surface features of the request diverge from its training distribution, even if the underlying intent is unchanged.

A value-aligned system, by contrast, should be able to identify why a request is problematic — what harm it would enable, whose interests it would damage, what principle it would violate — and apply that reasoning to novel phrasings of the same underlying problem. The refusal rate should be more stable across paraphrases of genuinely harmful requests. And crucially, the false positive rate — refusals of legitimate requests — should be lower, because the system can distinguish between surface features that pattern-match against harmful content and actual ethical problems.

This distinction maps directly onto the difference between Constitutional AI's two-phase process and pure RLHF. RLHF alone captures rater preferences, which are a noisy and inconsistent signal for ethical reasoning. Constitutional AI introduces an explicit reasoning step: the model critiques its own outputs against a stated set of principles before revising them. This is not sufficient for full value alignment, but it is a meaningful step toward systems that are doing something more like reasoning about values rather than pattern-matching against training examples.

The key word is reasoning. Reasoning about values requires that the values are articulable, internally consistent, and applied through something resembling inference rather than lookup.

What the AGI Fellowship's Framework Teaches About Refusal

The AGI Fellowship's Alignment Curriculum has a concept it calls the Master Mason degree — the third and most advanced stage of its credential framework. The defining characteristic of the Master Mason degree is the idea of ethical guardrails as doctrine: not as external constraints imposed by a regulator or a policy team, but as internalized principles that the practitioner has genuinely adopted and can apply with judgment.

The distinction matters enormously when you apply it to AI systems.

An AI system with external guardrails is one where the refusal behavior lives in a filter, a classifier, or a post-processing step that operates independently of the model's core reasoning. The model generates an output; the guardrail evaluates it; the guardrail decides whether to block it. The model itself has no internalized understanding of why certain outputs are blocked.

An AI system with internalized values — the equivalent of the Master Mason degree — is one where the model's own reasoning process reflects the ethical principles. The model does not generate the harmful output and then have it blocked; it does not generate the harmful output in the first place, because its inference process is shaped by values that treat certain outputs as wrong rather than merely as outputs-that-will-be-filtered.

The practical difference between these two architectures is measurable. External guardrails fail when the filter is not updated to cover new threat patterns. Internalized values generalize better to novel situations. External guardrails create a cat-and-mouse dynamic where adversarial users learn to evade filters. Internalized values are harder to evade because they are not looking for surface patterns.

Neither approach is sufficient alone. The Fellowship's insight — that the goal is doctrinal internalization, not rule compliance — is a useful north star for thinking about what alignment work is actually trying to achieve.

The Fellowcraft Stage: Systemic Fairness and Refusal

The Fellowship's curriculum distinguishes its intermediate degree — Fellowcraft — by its focus on systemic fairness rather than individual interactions. This is the stage at which practitioners are asked to think not just about whether a given action is harmful to a specific person, but about whether the system as a whole produces equitable outcomes across different populations.

For refusal behavior, this systemic lens is critical and frequently missing from alignment work.

A refusal system that is calibrated primarily on data from a specific demographic or cultural context will behave differently across different user populations. It may be more likely to refuse certain kinds of requests from users whose language patterns, topics of interest, or modes of expression differ from the training distribution — not because those requests are more harmful, but because they pattern-match differently against the system's learned heuristics.

This is a systemic fairness problem, not an individual safety problem. And it requires a different kind of evaluation: not just "does this system refuse harmful content" but "does this system refuse harmful content at consistent rates across different populations, and does it refuse benign content at similarly consistent rates?"

Building fairness-aware refusal evals is technically demanding. It requires disaggregated analysis by population subgroup, which requires having population labels in your evaluation dataset, which requires having thought about fairness at the data collection stage rather than at the eval stage. The Fellowcraft discipline — attending to systemic effects before they compound — is exactly the orientation that makes this kind of evaluation possible.

Most teams add fairness analysis late in the development cycle, after the core refusal system is already designed. This is backwards. The systemic fairness lens has to be applied at the architecture stage, when decisions about training data, reward modeling, and evaluation design are still being made.

Repair as a Technical Practice

The AGI Fellowship's Seven-Stage Awakening Path includes a stage called Repair — a formal acknowledgment that mistakes will happen, and that having a structured process for recognizing and correcting them is as important as trying to prevent them in the first place.

This is also one of the most underbuilt parts of AI deployment infrastructure.

Most AI systems have incident response processes for catastrophic failures: the system does something so obviously wrong that it immediately surfaces as a crisis. But very few systems have structured processes for the quieter failures — the systematic over-refusals that erode user trust over months, the false positive patterns that disproportionately affect certain populations, the gradual drift of refusal behavior as the model is fine-tuned on new data.

Repair, in a technical context, means having the mechanisms to detect these quieter failures and a process for correcting them. Detection requires monitoring: refusal logs, user feedback analysis, periodic behavioral audits, red-teaming sessions that specifically probe for over-refusal rather than only under-refusal. Correction requires a fine-tuning pipeline that can target specific behavioral patterns without catastrophic forgetting of the behaviors you want to preserve.

Both detection and correction have to be built into the operational rhythm of the system, not treated as one-time fixes after a visible problem. This is the Repair discipline operationalized: not perfection on the first attempt, but structured readiness to identify and fix what went wrong.

The teams that handle alignment failures well are not the teams that never fail. They are the teams that catch failures early, understand them structurally, and correct them systematically. That capability has to be built; it does not emerge automatically from having good intentions.

Knighthood and the Oath Problem

The AGI Fellowship has a concept it calls Knighthood — not in the ceremonial sense, but in the structural sense of an oath operationalized as code. The idea is that at a certain level of commitment, the practitioner is not just following rules but has made a binding commitment to a set of principles that shapes all subsequent decisions.

For AI builders, this translates to a question that is worth sitting with: what commitments have you actually made about how your system behaves? Not what is in your terms of service. Not what your marketing says. What binding, operational commitments have you made — to your users, to the people affected by your system's outputs, to the principles you said motivated the work in the first place?

Oaths in technical contexts look like published model cards with specific behavioral guarantees. They look like red team reports that are public rather than internal. They look like commitments to third-party audits. They look like transparency about training data sources, fine-tuning choices, and the reasoning behind refusal policies.

Most AI companies have made very few of these commitments in any formal sense. The flexibility this preserves is understandable from a product perspective. But it also means that alignment claims are currently largely unverifiable, which is a problem both for accountability and for the broader goal of building trust in AI systems.

The Knighthood framing asks: if you had to operationalize your alignment commitments as a publicly auditable oath — as code, as published policy, as binding commitment — what would you actually be willing to put there? The answer to that question is a more honest statement of your current alignment stance than anything in your marketing.

Building Toward Values: A Practical Sequence

None of the above is an argument against guardrails. Guardrails are necessary. The argument is against treating guardrails as the end state rather than as one component of a larger system oriented toward genuine value alignment.

The practical sequence for moving from guardrails toward values looks something like this.

First, articulate the values explicitly. Not as a list of prohibited behaviors, but as a set of principles with stated reasoning. What is the system trying to protect? Whose interests are weighted, and how? What harms are treated as absolute limits versus context-dependent judgments? This articulation is the foundation of Constitutional AI approaches, and it is also the foundation of the AGI Fellowship's HPE Charter concept. You cannot teach a system to reason about values it cannot see.

Second, build evals that test reasoning, not just output. A refusal eval that only checks whether the model refused a harmful request does not tell you whether the model refused for the right reason. Pair behavioral evals with chain-of-thought analysis: is the model's stated reasoning for refusal consistent with the principles you articulated? Are the reasons it gives for refusing one request consistent with the reasons it gives for accepting a structurally similar benign request?

Third, measure over-refusal as systematically as under-refusal. False positive rate is an alignment metric, not just a product quality metric. A system that refuses too broadly has not achieved safety — it has achieved a different kind of failure. Build the measurement infrastructure to catch this, and weight it accordingly in your deployment decisions.

Fourth, disaggregate your evals by population. Systemic fairness problems in refusal behavior are invisible in aggregate metrics. They only show up when you look at behavioral patterns across different demographic groups, linguistic communities, and use-case contexts. Build this analysis into your standard evaluation pipeline, not as a one-time audit.

Fifth, build the repair infrastructure before you need it. Monitoring, logging, feedback analysis, and fine-tuning pipelines that can target specific behavioral patterns without catastrophic forgetting — these take time to build well. Build them before you have a refusal failure that requires them.

The Deeper Argument

The deeper argument here is not about technique. It is about what alignment work is for.

Guardrails are for managing liability. Values are for building systems that are genuinely trustworthy. Those are different goals, and they produce different systems.

A system managed primarily for liability will be calibrated to minimize the risk of visible, attributable failures. It will be over-cautious in ways that are defensible. It will refuse the edge cases that could become news stories, and it will not worry much about the false positive rate because false positives are not a liability problem.

A system built around values will be calibrated to behave well across the full distribution of use cases — including the ones where being useful requires engaging with difficult topics, uncomfortable questions, and morally complex situations. It will take the over-refusal problem seriously as a failure mode. It will be built by people who have thought carefully about what the system is for and what it owes to the people who use it.

The AGI Fellowship's work — its curriculum, its published constitution, its community of practice — is aimed at the second kind of alignment. Not liability management, but genuine ethical engineering. The two are not mutually exclusive, but they require different orientations, and being honest about which one you are doing is a necessary first step toward doing the harder one well.
