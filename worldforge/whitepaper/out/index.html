Agents are moving from pilots into everyday software. Gartner predicts that 40 percent of enterprise applications will integrate task-specific agents by 2026, up from under 5 percent [1]. KPMG's Q3 2026 pulse found 62 percent of US organizations with revenue of $1 billion or more building, deploying or developing agents [2]. Sizing firms forecast the AI-agents market at US$48.3 billion [3] to US$52.62 billion [4] by 2030.

Governance trails deployment. The Register, covering Gartner's guidance on agent sprawl, reports that the average Global Fortune 500 enterprise is expected to run more than 150,000 agents by 2028 [5], yet only 15 percent of surveyed IT application leaders were considering, piloting or deploying fully autonomous agents [6], and 49 percent of KPMG respondents have defined high-risk use cases closed to autonomous agent decisions [7]. Gartner also predicts over 40 percent of agentic AI projects will be discontinued by the end of 2027 [8].

Rules are arriving. The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and became applicable on 2 August 2026 [9], with high-risk rules for areas such as critical infrastructure and education applying from 2 December 2027 [10]. NIST's voluntary AI RMF dates from January 2023 [11].

AGI Future Foundation PBC's stated mission is to ensure advanced AI is governed as a public good, with benefits shared widely and deployed toward humanity's most urgent challenges. Its management model gives agents research synthesis, task routing, proposal generation, operational coordination, progress tracking, monitoring developments, identifying collaborators and drafting grants, and keeps oversight, values alignment, final governance, accountability and ethical guardrails with humans.

The world network: 41 worlds in four families

Forty-one coming-soon worlds share one charter and one rulebook, and each family of worlds exists for a reason the registries record.

Moved since this explainer was written: Evals run: the text says 1,025, worldforge/out/index.json#totals.evals now reads 1,066. The prose below keeps the explainer's wording; the registry value is the current one.

The network starts as a list. my-fabric/worlds/index.json names 41 worlds, and WorldForge carries every one of them into worldforge/out/index.json, where each world gets its Master Cards, a blockout, a card-tagged glTF file and a manifest. All 41 are marked "coming soon". All 41 use the same management model, the Agent-managed DAO, with three steward seats and the same six actions reserved for people. Every world names its owner, AGI Future Foundation PBC, and carries the umbrella "Powered by AGI Corp".

The worlds fall into four families, and the family decides where a world's content comes from. The six PBC focus DAOs (Cancer, Robotics, Ecology, Climate, Education and Public Health) come from the Global Issue Focus in the foundation's mission, recorded under focus_domains in worlds/PBC.json. The 28 W3 LLC series worlds, slugs s04 to s35, come from the founder's series list in worlds/series.json. They have a name and a series number and nothing more yet, so worlds/focus.json records "focus and RP1 location not yet given" for each one. The three program-track worlds (Agentic Payments, Agents That Deploy Infrastructure and Autonomous Organizations) are built only from three track descriptions the founder pasted on 2026-09-28, and every zone and agent in them cites a line of that text. The four earlier worlds (Locator.X, Biota.blockchain, Empathic.X and Diagnostic.X) came before the shared template and draw their zones from their own source documents.

The families overlap with the W3 LLC series list, and the overlap is worth stating exactly. worlds/series.json lists 35 series. Thirty-three of them are among the 41 worlds: the 28 named-only worlds, the four earlier worlds (Series 2, 3, 8 and 18), and Robotics DAO, which is both a focus DAO and Series 30. The other two, Cognition.X (Series 1) and SmartCiti.X (Series 10), are fabrics that were already built. They are listed separately under "built" in my-fabric/worlds/index.json and are not counted in the 41.

A named-only world has no charter yet, so its spaces follow the shared PBC template: Charter Hall, Research Synthesis Lab, Proposal Forum, Operations Deck and Progress Observatory, plus the Steward Council. That is why 28 worlds look alike, and it is deliberate. The template shows the governance shape without inventing a mission. When the stewards write a focus line into worlds/focus.json, it flows into that world's spec. Until then the world's Omega Steward says plainly that its focus is not set.

Across the network WorldForge counts 939 Master Cards, 242 specialist agents, 246 zones and councils, 1,103 blockout primitives and 287 roads. It runs 1,025 evals, and none fails. A rule, not a hand choice, gives each family its own civic landmark style: setback towers for the 28 series worlds, a rotunda for the six focus DAOs, a twisting tower for the three tracks and an octagonal prism for the four earlier worlds. What makes this a network rather than 41 separate scenes is the shared rulebook, the shared building and avatar sets, and the handoffs between worlds. Each Omega Steward has one scripted handoff that sends a visitor to another world's Omega when its own world lacks the service asked for.

SmartCiti.X Master Cards: Volum, seven rights and six reserved actions

Every object in every world is a card that says what space it covers, who may do what there, and which decisions only people can make.

Moved since this explainer was written: Evals network-wide: the text says 1,025, worldforge/out/index.json#totals.evals now reads 1,066. The prose below keeps the explainer's wording; the registry value is the current one.

A Master Card is the unit of record in WorldForge. The card layer carries the SmartCiti.X name: every card is a SmartCiti.X Master Card. Each card carries an id, a kind, a parent, an owner (AGI Future Foundation PBC), the umbrella, a source citation, a Volum, a permission matrix and the list of reserved actions. A world's cards form one tree, root first, and the same tree is written into the world's glTF file. Every node carries its cardId and parentCardId in its extras, so a loader can index cards without trusting display names.

There are ten kinds. Across the 41 worlds the counts are 41 world, 41 plaza, 41 coordinator, 123 advisor, 41 gate, 41 council, 123 seat, 41 omega, 205 zone and 242 agent cards, 939 in all. Each world has one of each singular kind, three advisors and three seats. Zones and agents vary with the world's spec.

A Volum is the bounded 3-D region a card covers: a box given as a centre and half-extents in metres, Y up, in the world's frame. Volums do real work. They place buildings and avatars, and evals read them. One check confirms that each Omega Steward's Volum sits clear of the council's, so the Omega stands beside the council and never inside it. The geometry is synthetic placeholder massing, not surveyed land.

The permission matrix has seven rights: view, enter, build, operate, delegate, transfer and govern. Across all 939 cards it takes only a few shapes. View is public everywhere. Enter is public except on the council and its seats, which only stewards enter. Build and delegate belong to stewards. Operate belongs to stewards, and agents gain it in one form only, "agents:zone", on plaza, zone, coordinator and agent cards: an agent operates its own zone and nothing else. Transfer and govern belong to the steward quorum on every card.

On top of the matrix, every card carries the six actions the PBC reserves for people, copied verbatim from worlds/PBC.json: governance.final_vote, charter.amend, treasury.transfer, proposal.enact, windfall.distribute and steward.appoint. Each is held for the Steward Council, with a 2-of-3 quorum over 3 seats. That makes 5,634 reservation entries across the network, all identical. Agent, coordinator and advisor cards also list the same six under may_never. This is the working form of the line every cards file carries: agents operate; people govern.

WorldForge runs 25 evals on every world, 1,025 in all, and none fails. They include agents_hold_no_reserved_right, reserved_actions_verbatim, three_human_seats, no_agent_on_council, council_not_a_zone, cited and nothing_invented. Six deliberately broken fixtures must each fail exactly their own check, which shows the suite can tell a good world from a bad one. The cards are also drafted as ERC-721-style token metadata: 939 tokens, one per card, each ID the first 16 hex characters of sha256(cardId), with a Merkle root per world and one network root over the 41. Those drafts pass 537 checks. Their deployment record reads chain null, contract null, minted 0.

  • view: public on every card.
  • enter: public, except stewards only on council and seat cards.
  • build, delegate: stewards on every card.
  • operate: stewards; plus agents:zone on plaza, zone, coordinator and agent cards.
  • transfer, govern: steward-quorum on every card.

The PBC behind the worlds: structure, share classes and four programs

The worlds encode a charter written for AGI Future Foundation PBC, and this section separates what the charter describes from what the filed documents establish.

The foundation's mission, as recorded in worlds/PBC.json, is to ensure advanced AI is governed as a public good, with benefits shared widely and deployed toward humanity's most urgent challenges. Its management model is the Agent-managed DAO. The agent ledger and the keyring: who signed what

Every agent's place in its world is recorded in a hash-chained log that only steward signatures can extend, and the keyring is how real people will replace the test keys.

An agent never registers itself. agents/ledger.mjs writes agents/ledger/ledger.jsonl: one genesis entry followed by 283 registrations, one for each of the 41 coordinators and 242 specialists across the 41 worlds. A registration records the world, the agent id, its role, its zone and the source its role was taken from. Each entry is hashed with SHA-256 over its canonical JSON, which includes the previous entry's hash, so the entries form a chain back to a genesis whose previous hash is all zeros.

Each registration carries two Ed25519 signatures, from steward-1 and steward-2, meeting the 2-of-3 quorum. Verification walks the whole chain and fails on a broken link, on an entry altered after approval, on an entry with fewer than two valid steward signatures, and on any approval made by the agent itself. It is a blockchain-shaped log kept locally, not a public chain.

The signatures are labelled for what they are. The three steward keys are throwaway test keys, generated in memory on each run and never written to disk. Only their public halves are saved, in agents/ledger/stewards.json, under the label "test (no registered stewards yet)". No private key belongs in the project.

The keyring is the path from test keys to people. With agents/keyring.mjs a person generates their own Ed25519 key on their own machine, and the private key may only be written outside the repository and outside any git working tree. They submit a self-signed request naming the role, seat and scope. Because steward.appoint is reserved for people, the first council is seated by a board appointment record that people write and sign; the tool never writes one. After that, a steward request needs approvals from 2 of 3 registered stewards covering the scope. A scope holds at most three stewards, and one key never holds two seats. The registry is append-only and hash-chained, pinned by a HEAD line that gives the entry count and the last hash. A revocation is a new entry, never an edit.

Today the registry is empty: governance/keyring/registry.json has no entries, and HEAD reads 0 followed by an all-zero hash. Settlement code follows one rule. A world's signatures count from one council only: its registered stewards, else the foundation-wide council, else the labelled test stewards, never a mix. A keyring that fails verification closes the gate instead of falling back to test keys. The x402 keyring gate currently counts 0 registered councils and 42 on test keys.

A design note, agents/ledger/ONCHAIN.md, maps each ledger rule to an on-chain equivalent: a registry contract with one record per agent, a steward multisig in place of the 2-of-3 signatures, and functions only that multisig may call for the reserved actions. That contract has not been written or tested.

x402 payment rehearsal: priced, signed and held

The project rehearses the whole x402 exchange between agents with the real field names, and every flow stops before any value moves.

x402 is a payment-request pattern built on HTTP status 402, Payment Required. The rehearsal in agents/x402 copies its field names and headers (PAYMENT-REQUIRED, PAYMENT-SIGNATURE, PAYMENT-RESPONSE and the version 1 X-PAYMENT header) from the x402.org site and the coinbase/x402 README, read on 2026-10-06. The flow has not been tested against any facilitator.

Each world gets a catalogue of things a visitor's agent might pay for, priced in rehearsal units, which are not a currency. Every item cites the registry record it prices. When an agent asks for an item, the world answers with a 402 payment requirement whose scheme is "exact" and whose network, asset and payTo fields are null, because those are founder decisions and none is set in the repository. The visitor's avatar signs a payment payload with a per-run Ed25519 key, and only the public half is written. The world verifies the signature and checks the amount against a steward-set budget. Then it decides. Settlement needs two steward signatures, because treasury.transfer is reserved for people.

Cancer DAO shows the three outcomes. Its budget is 20 units and its catalogue has 7 items: six room sessions at 5 units each, one for each building including the Steward Council room, and one scaffold visit at 1 unit. A Charter Hall session signed by one steward is held. A Research Synthesis Lab session signed by two stewards would settle, but nothing is sent, because no rail is configured. A 70-unit scaffold request is refused as over budget.

Across 42 worlds (the 41 plus the SmartCiti.X Holodeck) the rehearsal lists 307 items and runs 126 flows: 42 held, 42 would-settle, 42 refused and 0 settled, with 0 failing checks. Each world file carries 14 checks. They confirm that payTo, asset and network stay unset, that prices are never a currency, that no flow settles, that no private key is written and that no personal data appears. Four deliberately broken self-tests, such as a filled-in payTo or a flow that settled, are each caught.

Agents are forbidden every money verb the rehearsal touches: set_price, accept_crypto, issue_invoice, move_funds and treasury.transfer. The crypto rail itself reads "not enabled until the board sets prices and counsel approves", with 9 prerequisites open. governance/advisory_rails.md lists what comes first. The Steward Council sets prices with 2 of 3 signatures, counsel and the founder decide which entity contracts, counsel approves standard terms, and every payer is identified and screened. For crypto it adds a money-transmission review, a tax treatment, wallet custody chosen by people so that moving funds takes more than one person, and a rule for mistaken or disputed agent payments.

  • would-settle: two steward signatures; nothing is sent because no rail, wallet, asset or network is set.

Agent swarms: one coordinator, a few specialists, one guard in the middle

Every world runs a small hub-hybrid swarm whose routing, redaction and containment are tested in code, with no model in the loop.

Each of the 41 WorldForge worlds has one agent swarm, written by agents/build_swarms.mjs to agents/swarms/<slug>.json. A swarm is a coordinator plus the specialist agents named in that world's own spec (worlds/<slug>/spec.json). Every specialist carries a name, a role, the zone it serves and a cited source; nothing in a swarm is typed in by hand. Across the 41 world swarms there are 242 specialists: 38 worlds field six, two field five and one fields four. Above the worlds sits the AGI Corp Concierge (agents/swarms/agi-corp.json), a directory that sends a visitor who names a world to that world's coordinator, choosing the longest name match so that a longer world name is not captured by a shorter one inside it.

The topology is called hub-hybrid: the coordinator supervises, and specialists hand off to each other swarm-style. The router is vendored unchanged from the SmartCiti.X Trade Craft Academy fabric (agents/fabric/router.mjs). Inside a world, a turn whose keywords clearly match one specialist goes straight to it; the router counts that as one model call. Anything fuzzy, unmatched or about another world goes to the coordinator, counted as two calls, and the decision is logged. A specialist may hand a turn to a peer, but never more than three handoffs per turn, and a turn that would return to an agent already visited ends with the coordinator. Routing keywords are derived, not written: each agent's name, role and zone purpose, minus common words, minus any word that two agents share, so each specialist routes on what is distinctively its own.

Every agent runs behind the same middleware (agents/fabric/agent.mjs). Before an agent sees a turn, five patterns redact text shaped like a US social security number, an email address, a phone number, a street address or a card number, and the agent's reply is redacted again on the way out. If a reply asks for one of the seven forbidden actions, the turn is refused, logged and counted; it is neither performed nor allowed to crash the hall. A second guard, pbcGuard in agents/build_swarms.mjs, removes any of the six actions the charter reserves for people (worlds/PBC.json reserved_for_humans) and appends "Held for the Steward Council: ... needs a human decision."

Each world swarm is held to nine evals, listed below. All 369 pass. Routing is measured on 170 questions written from each zone's purpose rather than from the agent's own wording: 40 worlds route every case to an accepted agent, and Autonomous Organizations routes 4 of 5 (0.8), exactly at the threshold. The concierge routes 41 of 41 world requests correctly.

The agents themselves are deterministic stubs: they answer with their role and hand off by keyword. That is deliberate. Routing, handoff, the recursion guard, redaction and containment can be measured without credentials or a model, and when a model is plugged into respond(), the middleware and the evals stay exactly as they are. Two adapters map the same topology onto other agent frameworks (agents/adapters/langgraph_swarm.py and sentient_agent.py); both are marked design only and have never been executed.

  • Routing accuracy of at least 0.8 on zone questions.
  • An unroutable turn ("hello there") lands on the coordinator, never on a guessed specialist.
  • PII is redacted in the middleware: a test email and phone number produce at least two redactions.
  • A forbidden action (dial.set_setpoint) is contained and counted, not performed.
  • Every agent cites a source.
  • An agent that tries proposal.enact and treasury.transfer is held for the stewards.
  • The world has a Steward Council with three human seats.
  • No agent sits on the Steward Council.
  • Each world has its own container, isolated like a Series LLC cell.

Metaprompts and the gates that hold them

Prompts are generated from the registries, tested like code, and the test suite is itself tested with prompts built to fail.

No model runs in this project, so a prompt here is a text artifact the registries produce, not something a person types. agents/metaprompt.mjs reads each world's spec, the charter (worlds/PBC.json), the built swarm and the middleware's own constants, and writes agents/prompts/<slug>.json. Each world gets a team charter that the whole swarm shares, a coordinator prompt carrying the routing table, one prompt per specialist (its zone, its role, its cited source and the peers it can hand off to), and a steward brief for every room console in the world's deep build. One more prompt serves the AGI Corp Concierge. That makes 571 prompts: 41 team charters, 41 coordinators, 242 specialists, 246 room-console briefs and 1 concierge. They run from 2,232 to 3,363 characters against a cap of 6,000.

Every prompt ends in the same common block: the world's name, umbrella, status and tagline; the management model ("Agent-managed DAO - agents operate, people govern"); what agents handle and what humans hold; the six reserved actions with the instruction to answer "Held for the Steward Council" and name the action; the seven forbidden actions; the five read-only surfaces; the rule to cite a source beside every claim; a refusal to ask for, store or repeat personal data; the not-advice line; and the world's known gaps, under the heading "do not fill these by invention".

evalPrompt in agents/metaprompt.mjs is the gate. Nine checks apply to every prompt, a tenth to every world prompt, and three more to every agent prompt; they are listed below. The last one is the subtle one. The coordinator routes on each specialist's keywords; if a specialist's own prompt never contains those words, it would be routed to on grounds it was never told about, and the two prompts would have drifted apart. The gate catches that drift before any model sees either prompt. All 6,435 checks across the 571 prompts pass.

A strict suite that passes everything proves little, so the suite is tested too. selfTest takes a good specialist prompt (the first specialist of Locator.X) and builds four broken copies: the umbrella dropped, treasury.transfer renamed, the source citation removed, and an email address appended. Each must fail exactly one check, its own. All four do. That is the difference between a suite that is strict and one that discriminates.

Each world also gets a spec metaprompt: the prompt that would regenerate worlds/<slug>/spec.json from the founder's one-line focus under the rules in worlds/SPEC.md. For 13 worlds the focus is set and the metaprompt is ready. For the other 28, the series worlds, the slot reads "<NOT SET - a founder action; do not invent one>" and the metaprompt is marked not ready rather than filled with a guess. Advisors get no prompt at all, by contract: they are scripted guides, not AI models.

  • Carries the umbrella, "Powered by AGI Corp".
  • Lists every one of the six reserved actions, and says they are held for the Steward Council.
  • Lists every one of the seven forbidden actions.
  • States the management model and refuses personal data.
  • Carries the not-advice line, word for word.
  • Is at most 6,000 characters and contains no PII, API-key, private-key or wallet-address shaped strings.
  • Names the world and its status (world prompts).
  • Cites the agent's own source, names its zone, and contains every routing keyword the coordinator uses for it (agent prompts).

Synthetic voices: what is voiced and what is not

Every voice is Kokoro text-to-speech run locally, every line says it is synthetic, and the manifests list only what was actually rendered.

Counts written from the registries when this page was built: the voicing and beat counts in this section are read from worldforge/out/voices/manifest.json#totals; worldforge/out/avatars/index.json#totals; agents/hive/index.json#totals; agents/hive/dialogues.json#totals and evidence/heartbeat.log, not from the explainer text; the explainer's earlier wording is kept in worldforge/whitepaper/out/build.json under rebuilt.

All audio in this part of the project comes from Kokoro v1.0 (kokoro-onnx), an Apache-2.0 text-to-speech model that runs locally. No account, key or credit is involved and nothing is sent anywhere. agents/hive.mjs lists 28 English Kokoro voices and leaves one out, am_santa, because it is a character voice and an Omega imitates no one. That leaves a pool of 27. Omegas take voices at a stride of 10, which shares no factor with 27, so neighbouring Omegas sound different and every voice is used; all 27 appear across the 44 Omegas. Avatar rows pick from the same pool by hashing the avatar id, and all 27 appear across the 447 rows.

There are three bodies of audio, and each has its own record (listed below). The hive's own lines are nearly complete; the dialogues and the avatar intros are a fraction done. All of it is stored the same way: mono AAC at 48 kbps and 24 kHz in an .mp4 container with faststart, loudness-normalised by ffmpeg in two passes to -16 LUFS integrated and -1.5 dBTP true peak.

Three contracts keep the record honest. First, a line counts as spoken only when its file exists and the manifest shows it was rendered from the same words, voice and speed; a hive line must also run 11.5 seconds or less, and a dialogue line is fitted to 18 seconds. Second, the avatar registry stores only the expected audio path, never a voiced or exists flag, because such a flag would go stale as lines are rendered; viewers check for the file at view time and otherwise use the browser's speech with the row's pitch, rate and language, labelled synthetic voice. Third, the voice manifest lists only lines actually rendered, so a missing entry means not voiced. When a line's text or voice changes, its entry is dropped and its file deleted before it is rendered again, so a viewer never plays an outdated line.

Every line discloses itself. The avatar intros end with "You are hearing a synthetic voice", the Omega greeting adds "I am an agent, not a person", and the voicing script skips and reports any row whose intro does not say so. Nothing plays on its own: the console builders check that no player autoplays and that playback starts only when a person presses play.

Rendering is polite to the machine it shares. The intro renderer (worldforge/voice/voice_intros.py) uses at most two threads, is time-boxed and resumable, and sleeps through the other team's film render windows. Hive audio is rebuilt from the registry and never committed; the repository ignores agents/hive/voices/.

Buildings: 246 shells, 246 fit-outs and one texture library

Every zone in every world, the Steward Council hall included, stands inside a textured building whose door lines up with the room behind it.

Each world's spec names its zones. The deep-world builder turns every zone into a room 10 m wide, 8 m deep and 3.6 m high, and my-fabric/spaces.py wraps that room twice: an exterior shell (a facade with a window grid and mullions, an entrance portal, a plinth with steps, a roof slab with parapet and rooftop plant, and a sign band) and an interior fit-out (a patterned floor with an inlay border, wall panelling, skirting and a dado rail, a coffered ceiling with light panels, and the furniture and fixtures of the room's kind). A room's kind comes from its own purpose text, matched by build_deep.kind_of() against seven kinds: forum (113 rooms), lab (74), ops (37), studio (13, also the fallback), desk (4), clinic (3) and library (2). Every texture is procedural, made with numpy and Pillow from a fixed seed, so the same registries always produce the same bytes. No building is modelled on a real one.

The textures are PBR sets: albedo, a normal map, and an ORM map that packs occlusion, roughness and metalness into one image. The hero facade of each kind is 2048 px; floor, wall and ceiling are 1024 px. That is 84 library textures (7 kinds, 4 surfaces, 3 maps), 21 of them at 2048 px. Each set is embedded once, in its kind's two hero models (14 shared models, 10,434,416 bytes), and every room of that kind in every world points at those two models. A room's own GLB keeps only what is unique to it: the sign band lettered with the zone's name (246 sign textures), the world's accent colour, the console and the placard. All room models together come to 2,151,432 bytes, and the whole layer to 12.6 MB. The sets are embedded rather than linked because a GLB that points at an external image fails this repository's validator loop, and the RP1 exporter and publish bundle carry .glb files only.

The doors are aligned by construction and then checked. All 246 exteriors carry the same opening: 2.4 m wide and 2.8 m high, centred on the room's front wall at (0, 0, 4.0) in the model frame. The spaces check asserts that each shell and fit-out is a child node at its room's origin with no rotation or scale, and that every room's door faces the plaza centre. It casts rays through the doorway for each of the seven kinds: they must pass clear through shell, fit-out and room, while rays beside the doorway must hit the facade. It also checks that every sign shows its zone's registry name, that no texture exceeds 2048 px, that a rebuild is byte-for-byte identical, and that every spaces model passes the Khronos validator with 0 errors and 0 warnings. Each WorldForge zone card links its building through a building block, and the buildings_resolve eval checks that link.

Every asset is written by a deterministic Python or Node generator, re-checked by that generator's --check mode, and validated against the Khronos glTF 2.0 specification.

The files under worldforge/out and my-fabric/worlds/models are generator output, not hand edits. Geometry and textures come from Python 3 builders using numpy and Pillow: spaces.py, scenes.py, build_env.py, build_deep.py, build_landmarks.py and build_avatars.py. The card layer and the registries come from Node builders: worldforge/build.mjs, avatars.mjs, onchain.mjs, and the agent builders under agents/. Each has a --check mode that rebuilds in memory, compares the result byte for byte with what is on disk, runs its evals, and exits 1 when anything is stale or failing. The same registries always produce the same bytes. The spaces and onchain builders both test that directly.

Every model is binary glTF 2.0 (GLB), Y-up, in metres, with its textures embedded so that each file stands alone. WorldForge writes one GLB per world: blockout massing built from one shared unit-cube mesh, in which every node carries its cardId and parentCardId in glTF extras. A spatial fabric is a JSON document in the MSF format. It holds a container name, the modules the client runs (here a map.wasm), primary lighting, and a Data.Scene tree. In that tree every node has a name, a Resource.sReference pointing at a GLB, a transform, and its children. The deep fabrics in my-fabric/worlds/<slug>/deep.json hold 41 worlds and 246 rooms. Forecourt nodes hang under the room's exterior node, and avatar nodes stand in the rooms and on the plaza. The Sneeze native path: gates 1.1 to 1.4

WorldForge carries each world toward MetaversalCorp's Sneeze engine through five gates: two are done, one is prepared with a decoder checked against the engine's real headers, and two are open.

The prose below keeps the explainer's wording; the registry value is the current one.

The native path runs in five steps: generated core, card-tagged GLB, Sneeze Scene Object Model (SOM) mount plan, native load, rendered frame. Gate 1.1, a generated core for every world, is done for all 41. Gate 1.2, a GLB written, parsed and checked against its card tree, is done in JavaScript and confirmed by the Khronos validator. Gate 1.2n, a native fastgltf load in a Sneeze host, is open. Gate 1.3, the SOM mount plan, is prepared but not mounted. Gate 1.4, a mounted scene rendered in the Sneeze viewport with a frame captured, is open.

Each world's sneeze-mount-plan.json lists its nodes parents first, ready for CONTAINER::Node_Open. The 41 plans hold 2,042 nodes: 939 card nodes of class MAP_OBJECT_TERRESTRIAL (72) and 1,103 resource nodes of class MAP_OBJECT_PHYSICAL (73). All the resource nodes share one unit-cube resource whose URL is 35 bytes, well inside the 127 usable bytes of the engine's 128-byte sReference field. Every node keeps its cardId and an extras block (its kind and source, or the zone it belongs to) in the plan.

worldforge/native holds an RMCOBJECT decoder written against the real headers: Sneeze 0008d08, Map 0e6d26a and RMAP 0fd7efb. The repositories were cloned to a temporary folder, not into this repository. The engine's native node record is a 504-byte POD, and static_asserts pin its layout, so any upstream change breaks the check. The decoder compiles with clang++ -std=c++17 -fsyntax-only in two configurations. A smoke run over all 41 plans and 2,042 nodes found 0 decode errors and 0 invariant failures.

The conformance script turned up real mismatches. On the native route there are 267 violations, all of one kind: names of 48 UTF-16 units where the engine allows 47, since it keeps a final NUL. Clamping would turn "· Powered by AGI Corp" into "· Powered by AGI Cor". The fix belongs in worldforge/build.mjs, whose 48-character limit was set for Artemis. On the JSON (MSF Data) route there are 1,529 violations on 1,139 nodes, because that decoder copies UTF-8 bytes one per unit: 939 names are non-ASCII, 323 are over 47 bytes and 267 are over 47 units. The plans are Y-up and Sneeze is Z-up, so the decoder converts each position to (x, −z, y) and conjugates each rotation. Sent unconverted, every world would mount on its side. The native record has no slot for card identity, extras or bounds, so those travel beside it. Reading the source also turned up eight upstream defects and doc drifts, among them a JSON name copy that can overrun its 48-unit field. They are recorded, not patched. The --check mode of mount_plan_conformance.mjs exits 1 until the name limit is fixed.

  • Gartner's newsroom pages returned a bot check to automated retrieval, which was not bypassed. Recheck the wording against Gartner's own pages before publication.
  • McKinsey's State of AI pages timed out on automated retrieval, so no McKinsey figures are used.
  • Survey figures describe specific samples, not whole populations. For example, KPMG's Q3 2026 pulse covered 314 US leaders at organizations with $1 billion or more in revenue, fielded July 24 to Aug. 25, 2026; Gartner's autonomy figure comes from 360 IT application leaders in 2025.

FAQ

Questions people ask

Are any of these worlds open to the public?

No. All 41 are marked "coming soon". None is published on RP1's Earth, which is the founder's call, and none is mounted in Sneeze. Four have an RP1 anchor; 37 do not.

Can an agent move money, vote or change the charter?

No. Those actions are among the six reserved for people, held on every card for a 2-of-3 Steward Council. Agents hold one extra right, operating their own zone. An eval on every world checks that no agent card holds a reserved right.

Who are the stewards?

Nobody yet. Every council's three seats read seated: null, and the keyring has no registered keys. The first stewards are seated by a board appointment record that people write and sign. Until then the ledger is signed with labelled test keys.

Are the Omega Stewards live AI agents?

They are defined, not seated. Each has a written persona, a synthetic voice that says it is synthetic, and scripted answers grounded in cited records. No person has seated an agent in any of them, and none takes action.

Are the advisors AI?

No. The Host Advisor, Steward Liaison and Alignment Advisor are scripted guides. Every line they can say is written with a citation or read from a registry path.

Is anything on a blockchain?

No. The 939 Master Cards are drafted as ERC-721-style metadata with Merkle roots, and the deployment record reads chain null, contract null, minted 0. The agent ledger is a local hash-chained log; its on-chain contract is a design note only.

Has any payment happened?

No. The x402 rehearsal ran 126 flows across 42 worlds and settled none. Prices are rehearsal units, and no wallet, asset, network or rail is configured.

Why do 28 of the worlds look alike?

They are W3 LLC series worlds with a name and a number only. Rather than invent a mission, they use the shared PBC template until the stewards write a focus line into worlds/focus.json.

What legal entity stands behind the worlds?

The filed entity is a California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation. The site's three-tier description, the W3 LLC series and the Class A, B and C shares are recorded as claimed or open, and where the share classes sit is for counsel to decide. This paper is not legal advice.

Where do the numbers in this paper come from?

Each fact names the registry file and field it was read from, and the build that writes this file re-reads every value and stops if a registry has changed. Nothing is estimated.

Are the voices real people?

No. Every rendered line is Kokoro text-to-speech run locally, and each greeting says it is a synthetic voice. Where a line has not been rendered, viewers fall back to a labelled browser voice.

Does an AI model answer visitors today?

No. Every agent is a deterministic stub that answers with its role and hands off by keyword, so routing, redaction and containment can be tested without credentials. The prompts a model would receive exist and are tested; the adapters that would connect a model are design only and have never been run.

Are the Omega Stewards seated, or people?

Neither. All 44 are defined, not seated, and each says in its greeting that it is an agent with a synthetic voice, not a person. Names are role titles; no persona imitates a real person.

Whose voices are these?

No one's. Every voice is Kokoro text-to-speech run locally from scripted text, and every line says the voice is synthetic. A character voice in the pool was excluded for that reason.

How much is actually voiced?

173 of 176 Omega lines, 170 of 352 dialogue lines and 159 avatar intros (41 coordinators, 41 specialists and 77 advisors). 247 of the 406 non-Omega intros are not voiced yet: 46 of 123 advisors and 201 of 242 specialists. The registries store only what was rendered. (Counts read from the voice manifest, the avatar registry and the hive registries when this page was built.)

Why do 123 avatars have no prompt?

They are advisors: scripted guides that read fixed records. By contract they are not AI models, so they get no persona prompt and no metaprompt binding.

How do you know the prompts have not drifted from the swarms?

evalPrompt checks that every specialist's prompt contains each keyword the coordinator routes on, and the whole suite is proved able to fail by four broken prompts that each fail exactly their own check. The PROMPTS field of the heartbeat reports 0 failing across 571 prompts.

Is any of this deployed, minted or mounted?

No. Nothing is deployed or minted on any chain (worldforge/out/onchain/index.json: not deployed, not minted). The scenes are not mounted in Sneeze. Only 4 of 41 worlds have an RP1 anchor (worldforge/out/index.json totals.anchored), and whether any world is published on RP1 is the founder's call.

Why did Autonomous Organizations score 0.8 on routing?

It routed 4 of its 5 zone questions to an accepted agent. That meets the 0.8 threshold exactly and is recorded as it was measured, not rounded up.

What stops a stale page or a broken build from looking green?

Every console and registry has a --check that recomputes its source stamp, and the heartbeat stops itself after two REDs in a row. The most recent RED, a stale robotics pitch console, was rebuilt and the next beat was GREEN.

Are any of these worlds live on RP1 today?

No. Six worlds pass every publication check and 38 are blocked only by a missing anchor (rp1/publish/manifest.json), but nothing has been uploaded, claimed, installed or published. Choosing anchors and publishing public content are the founder's decisions.

Has a world been loaded or rendered in Sneeze?

No. Gates 1.1 and 1.2 are done, and gate 1.3 has a decoder that compiles against Sneeze's real headers and decodes all 2,042 plan nodes. No Sneeze host has been built, nothing has been mounted, and no frame has been rendered (worldforge/native/RMCOBJECT_MAPPING.md §9).

Are the buildings, towers or landscapes modelled on real places?

No. Every building, forecourt, biome and landmark is procedural and generated from the project's own registries. The landmark GLBs declare this in their root node, and each environment file includes an honesty line.

Why embed textures in every GLB instead of sharing image files?

A GLB that points at an external image fails the repository's validator loop, and the RP1 exporter and publish bundle carry only .glb files. So each kind's full texture set is embedded once in two shared hero models that every room references. The whole spaces layer comes to 12.6 MB (my-fabric/worlds/spaces_index.json).

Has anything been minted or deployed on a blockchain?

No. worldforge/out/onchain holds draft ERC-721 metadata and sha256 Merkle roots for 939 cards, with chain and contract set to null, 0 minted and 0 transactions. Minting or transferring a Master Card is reserved for a 2-of-3 Steward Council, and no council is seated.

Why does the Sneeze conformance check report 267 violations if gate 1.3 is prepared?

"Prepared" means the plans and the decoder exist and are checked. The 267 violations are all one rule: names of 48 UTF-16 units where Sneeze allows 47. The decoder clamps them safely, but the real fix is a separate 47-unit limit in worldforge/build.mjs, and until then the conformance --check exits 1 on purpose. [Registry update since this answer was written: 267 is now 0 (worldforge/native/conformance-report.json#totals.native.violations).]

Is the Neon database running?

No. neon/ holds the schema and seed for 9 tables and 1,522 rows, built and checked offline. It is applied only after the founder authorises the Neon connector, and the connection string stays in the environment, never in the repository.

How can a reader check a number in this paper?

Every fact names its source file. The generators' --check modes rebuild their outputs and fail when the files on disk disagree, and evidence/heartbeat.log records each beat's results.

Do the market figures on this page show demand for SmartCiti.X?

No. They are forecasts and survey results about adjacent markets, cited with their publishers and dates. AGI Future Foundation PBC and SmartCiti.X have no revenue, users, customers or partnerships to report.

A condition on which the heartbeat halts instead of continuing: RED twice in a row, low disk, or a server it cannot start.
Swarm
One world's team of agents: a coordinator plus the specialists named in that world's spec, written to agents/swarms/<slug>.json.
Test keys
Throwaway Ed25519 steward keys made in memory on each run. Only their public halves are saved, labelled "test (no registered stewards yet)".
Volum
The bounded 3-D region a card covers: a box given by a centre and half-extents in metres, Y up, in the world's frame.
W3 LLC series world
A world for one numbered series cell of W3 LLC. Twenty-eight of them have a name only and use the shared template.
Windfall Clause
The PBC program to redistribute profits above a set threshold globally. Its threshold, share and recipient are not stated.
x402
A payment-request pattern built on HTTP status 402 (Payment Required), rehearsed here with its real field names and no settlement.
